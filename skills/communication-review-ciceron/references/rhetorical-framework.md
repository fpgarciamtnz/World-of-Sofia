# Rhetorical Framework

Use this reference when a communication review needs deeper analysis than the default compact report.

## Core Principle

Review the message by rhetorical fitness: the fit among goal, speech act, audience, emotional cost, evidence, frame, style, and ethical constraints. A message is not merely a string of sentences. It is an attempt to change judgment, feeling, relationship, or action.

## Classical Spine

The Cicero-inspired commitments are practical:

- Rhetoric exists for a purpose beyond verbal elegance.
- Speech should fit audience, occasion, and stakes.
- Substance, order, style, and delivery belong together.
- The speaker's projected character and role affect uptake.
- A message should be judged by what it enables in the situation.

Use Cicero as orientation, not as the whole apparatus. Modern communication review also needs speech-act analysis, implication, audience constraints, argument structure, face-work, framing, emotional impact, and reader-centered revision.

## Eight Checks

1. **Goal inference**: Ask what practical change would count as success. Do not confuse topic with goal.
2. **Speech-act classification**: Identify what the message does by being said. Surface form can differ from function.
3. **Audience inference**: Identify the receiver whose uptake matters, not every possible reader.
4. **Emotional impact analysis**: Separate the speaker's emotional signal from the receiver's likely emotional fallout.
5. **Rhetorical-position analysis**: Identify the role claimed by the speaker and whether wording supports that role.
6. **Tension detection**: Look for misfit between goal, act, frame, evidence, tone, audience, and emotional cost.
7. **Effectiveness scoring**: Score dimensions only when useful; avoid false precision.
8. **Reformulation strategy**: Make the smallest revision that improves fit.

## Useful Distinctions

- **Observed text**: What the message literally says or strongly signals.
- **Inferred intention**: The likely goal or communicative act, stated with confidence.
- **Likely uptake**: How a receiver may understand or feel about the message.
- **Rhetorical tension**: A practical misfit, such as "I want collaboration" paired with status pressure or blame.
- **Face threat**: Wording that risks humiliating, cornering, dismissing, or lowering the receiver's standing.
- **Frame**: The conceptual angle the language activates, such as blame, repair, negotiation, duty, care, urgency, or fairness.

## Guardrails

- Do not turn every indirect message into manipulation. Indirectness can be politeness, uncertainty, or relationship management.
- Do not turn every firm boundary into aggression. Some messages should remain direct.
- Do not treat emotional pressure as effectiveness by itself.
- Do not erase accountability when rewriting.
- Do not import therapy language unless the user asks for it or it is plainly useful.
- Prefer reader-aware clarity over ornamental eloquence.

## Revision Moves

Use the smallest effective move first:

- Make the request explicit.
- Add the missing grounds.
- Replace evaluative pseudo-observations with concrete observations.
- Reorder context before demand.
- Name the constraint instead of implying blame.
- Preserve the boundary but remove unnecessary insult or contempt.
- Replace vague concern with a concrete next step.
- Shift from accusation to impact when repair is the goal.
- Shift from softness to clarity when action is the goal.
