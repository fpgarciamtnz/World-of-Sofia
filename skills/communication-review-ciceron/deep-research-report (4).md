# Cicero-Inspired Rhetorical Intent Reviewer Concept Guide

## Executive summary

A strong **Cicero-inspired Rhetorical Intent Reviewer** should treat messages as purposeful acts aimed at changing judgment, feeling, or action, not merely as strings of sentences. Cicero’s mature rhetorical theory is useful here because it is practical, audience-facing, and ethically burdened: the ideal orator must understand human affairs, not just verbal ornament, and the best discourse should **instruct, delight, and move**. His early *De Inventione* also gives a compact architecture for revision through invention, arrangement, style, memory, and delivery. citeturn22view0turn10search7turn27search0turn12search12

But Cicero is not enough on his own. He does not give a modern theory of speech acts, context-sensitive intention recognition, conversational implicature, cognitive framing, or empirical emotional processing. For those tasks, the best complements are Austin and Searle on what an utterance *does*; Grice on what is implied but not literally said; Bitzer on exigence, audience, and constraints; Toulmin on claim-support structure; Peirce and Dewey on inquiry and revisable judgment; Goffman on face and self-presentation; Lakoff on framing; Kahneman and Tversky on reference points and framing effects; Nonviolent Communication on observation, feelings, needs, and requests; and Orwell, Zinsser, and Williams on revision for clarity and reader uptake. citeturn24view5turn24view4turn28search2turn26view0turn30view0turn32view2turn33view2turn35search5turn31view0turn38view0turn37view0turn22view8turn22view9turn22view10

The central design conclusion is this: the skill should not ask only whether a message is “good.” It should ask whether the message is **rhetorically fit** for its goal, audience, emotional consequences, evidential structure, and ethical constraints. That means the workflow should move through eight conceptual checks: goal inference, speech-act classification, audience inference, emotional impact analysis, rhetorical-position analysis, contradiction/tension detection, effectiveness scoring, and reformulation strategy. Any score the skill produces should be a derived rubric of these dimensions, not a mystical one-number judgment. citeturn26view0turn27search0turn33view2turn22view10turn22view11

## Classical anchor in Cicero

The most useful Ciceronian anchor texts for this skill are **three** works. *De Inventione* gives the classical parts of rhetorical work: invention, arrangement, expression, memory, and delivery. *De Oratore* develops a richer model of the ideal orator as someone who has mastered law, philosophy, and especially “human life and manners.” *Orator* and *De Optimo Genere Oratorum* sharpen the evaluative standard: the orator should instruct, delight, and move the audience, with moving them treated as indispensable. citeturn12search12turn22view0turn10search7turn27search0

From Cicero, the reviewer should therefore inherit five durable commitments. First, rhetoric is **practical** and exists for something beyond itself. Second, discourse must be judged by **fitness to audience and occasion**, not by elegance alone. Third, style, order, and substance belong together. Fourth, the speaker’s public standing and character matter; rhetoric is not reducible to information transfer. Fifth, discourse should be evaluated by what it actually enables in a situation. Modern scholarship on Cicero’s decorum reinforces this picture by showing that audience adaptation is not merely tactical but tied to judgment and standards of appropriateness. citeturn26view0turn27search7turn27search15

Just as important is what **not** to take from Cicero. He gives orientation, not a complete analytic apparatus. He does not distinguish locutionary, illocutionary, and perlocutionary force in Austin’s sense; he does not provide Toulmin’s explicit claim/warrant/backing map; he does not offer a modern theory of indirect speech acts, framing effects, or emotional inference. So Cicero should function as the **classical spine** of the reviewer, while modern theories provide the operational joints. citeturn24view5turn30view0turn38view0turn37view0

## Workflow concepts by step

### Goal inference

**Core idea.** The Ciceronian starting point is that rhetoric is for something: the orator should instruct, delight, and move, and rhetoric is pragmatic discourse that exists to modify judgment or action. Goal inference should therefore ask not “what is this about?” but “what state of affairs would satisfy the speaker?” citeturn27search0turn26view0

**Key supporting theories or thinkers.** Bitzer clarifies that rhetoric arises from an **exigence** addressed to an audience under constraints. Peirce treats inquiry as a response to the irritation of doubt, and Dewey defines reflective thought as disciplined consideration of grounds and consequences. Together, these improve Cicero by making goal inference explicitly problem-centered and revisable. citeturn26view0turn32view0turn33view2

**Essential concepts.**  
An **exigence** is the problem or urgency discourse aims to modify. A **rhetorical audience** is the subset of hearers capable of acting on the message. **Inquiry** begins where there is live doubt, and **reflective thought** considers both supporting grounds and downstream consequences. citeturn26view0turn26view1turn32view0turn33view2

**Conceptual role in the skill.** The AI should infer a ranked list of candidate goals: informing, requesting, apologizing, warning, aligning, setting a boundary, negotiating, persuading, de-escalating, or preserving face. It should prefer the most **action-relevant** goal supported by wording, situation, and implied outcome. citeturn26view0turn33view2

**Common pitfalls.** The main errors are reducing goal to topic, assuming every message aims to persuade, and confusing a writer’s explicit slogan with the practical end of the text. Peirce and Dewey both help here: a real interpretive model begins with live doubt and revises itself when later evidence fits better. citeturn32view0turn33view2

**Minimal prioritized reading.** Read first: Cicero, *De Optimo Genere Oratorum* or *Orator* for the instruct-delight-move standard; Bitzer, “The Rhetorical Situation” for exigence, audience, and constraints; Peirce, “The Fixation of Belief”; Dewey, *How We Think*. citeturn27search0turn26view0turn32view0turn33view2

### Speech-act classification

**Core idea.** A message is not just content; it is an act. In classical terms, rhetoric performs work in the world. In modern terms, this becomes the question of **illocutionary force**: what is the speaker doing by saying this? citeturn26view0turn24view5

**Key supporting theories or thinkers.** Austin distinguishes **locutionary**, **illocutionary**, and **perlocutionary** acts. Searle systematizes speech acts into families such as assertives, directives, commissives, expressives, and declarations. Grice and later work on implicature explain how a literal question can function as a request, suggestion, warning, or reproach. citeturn24view5turn13search4turn24view4turn28search0

**Essential concepts.**  
A **locutionary act** is the utterance with its propositional content. An **illocutionary act** is the act performed in uttering it, such as stating, promising, or warning. A **perlocutionary act** is the effect on the audience, such as persuading or alarming. **Indirect speech acts** and **implicatures** arise when intended force exceeds literal form. citeturn24view5turn24view4turn28search2

**Conceptual role in the skill.** The reviewer should classify the dominant act and any subordinate acts. For example, “Can you send the file today?” is literally a question but often functionally a request; “I’m surprised this happened again” may perform disappointment, blame, escalation, or pressure depending on context. citeturn24view4turn28search2

**Common pitfalls.** Literalism is the biggest error. Another is over-interpretation: not every implication is a fully intended secondary speech act. The skill should therefore separate **surface form**, **best-supported force**, and **confidence in that inference**. citeturn24view4turn28search0

**Minimal prioritized reading.** Read first: Austin, *How to Do Things with Words*; Searle, “A Taxonomy of Illocutionary Acts” and “Indirect Speech Acts”; the SEP entries on speech acts and implicature as compact maps. citeturn24view5turn13search4turn24view4turn28search0turn28search2

### Audience inference

**Core idea.** The classical anchor is **decorum**: speech should be fitted to the audience, subject, and occasion. Cicero’s rhetorical ideal assumes that different publics require different treatment, and that the orator must understand human life and manners to speak well. citeturn27search7turn27search15turn10search7

**Key supporting theories or thinkers.** Bitzer sharpens audience inference by distinguishing the **rhetorical audience** from everyone who merely overhears the message. Goffman adds a relational layer: interaction is structured by presentation of self and face. Williams brings the reader-centered correction that writers may know what they mean, but readers decide whether they have said it well. citeturn26view1turn22view7turn22view10

**Essential concepts.**  
A **rhetorical audience** is the audience capable of acting. **Constraints** are the social, institutional, emotional, or situational conditions shaping uptake. **Decorum** is appropriate fittingness. In this guide, a **bounded audience** means the specific audience segment whose response actually matters for the message’s success. citeturn26view1turn27search15turn22view10

**Conceptual role in the skill.** The AI should infer audience from cues such as assumed knowledge, pronouns, register, jargon, requested action, power distance, and institutional context. It should ask: is this written for a boss, peer, customer, public, friend, offended partner, committee, or mixed audience, and which audience is rhetorically decisive? citeturn26view1turn22view10

**Common pitfalls.** The most serious mistakes are treating “everyone” as the audience, confusing bystanders with decision-makers, and reading tone as personality rather than adaptation to context. A terse executive summary and a consoling private message can both be fit, but to different audiences and constraints. citeturn26view1turn27search15

**Minimal prioritized reading.** Read first: Cicero, *De Oratore*; scholarship on Cicero’s decorum; Bitzer on rhetorical audience and constraints; Williams, *Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace*. citeturn22view0turn27search15turn26view1turn22view10

### Emotional impact analysis

**Core idea.** Classical rhetoric never treated emotion as optional. Cicero’s standard that discourse should **move** the audience makes emotional impact central, and in the classical tradition pathos is one of persuasion’s major modes. citeturn27search0turn29search1

**Key supporting theories or thinkers.** Nonviolent Communication helps separate observation from evaluation and needs from strategies, which is crucial for parsing emotional content without collapsing into accusation. Goffman adds face-threat analysis. Kahneman and Tversky explain why emotionally equivalent content can land very differently when framed relative to gains, losses, and reference points. citeturn37view0turn37view1turn35search5turn38view0turn38view1turn38view2

**Essential concepts.**  
An **observation** is what happened without evaluative overlay. A **feeling** is an emotion or bodily signal rather than a disguised judgment. A **need** is the underlying life-serving concern, distinct from a specific strategy. **Face** is the positive social value someone claims, and **loss framing** often produces stronger reactions than gain framing because losses loom larger than gains. citeturn37view0turn37view1turn35search5turn38view2

**Conceptual role in the skill.** The reviewer should estimate emotional effects on both sides: what the speaker expresses, what the audience is likely to feel, what face is threatened, and whether the wording invites defensiveness, shame, clarity, solidarity, urgency, or trust. It should distinguish emotional **signal** from emotional **fallout**. citeturn37view1turn35search5turn38view1

**Common pitfalls.** The skill should not pretend to read minds. It should avoid turning every value conflict into trauma language, confusing “I feel disrespected” with a pure feeling report, or assuming that stronger emotion is necessarily more persuasive. NVC is particularly useful because it shows how often apparent feelings are really judgments about others. citeturn37view1

**Minimal prioritized reading.** Read first: Cicero on moving the audience; official NVC materials on the four components and the feelings/needs distinction; Goffman, “On Face-Work”; Tversky and Kahneman, “The Framing of Decisions and the Psychology of Choice.” citeturn27search0turn37view0turn23view6turn35search5turn38view0

### Rhetorical-position analysis

**Core idea.** Classical rhetoric asks not only what is said but **who the speaker is presenting themselves as**. In practice, this is a question of ethos, persona, and decorum: the speaker’s projected role, standing, and relation to the audience. citeturn22view0turn27search15turn29search1

**Key supporting theories or thinkers.** Goffman is decisive here because he treats interaction as presentation and face maintenance. Classical ethos helps specify credibility and trust. Lakoff contributes by showing that language activates frames and values, so rhetorical position is not only social but also moral and conceptual. citeturn22view7turn35search5turn31view0turn23view2

**Essential concepts.**  
**Ethos** concerns projected credibility or character. A **line**, in Goffman’s sense, is the position a person takes in interaction. **Face** is the value attached to that line. A **frame** is the conceptual structure that organizes how an issue is being understood. In practical reviewing, rhetorical position means the role claimed by the text: peer, authority, ally, critic, supplicant, mediator, expert, or witness. citeturn35search5turn31view0turn23view2

**Conceptual role in the skill.** The AI should infer whether the message is speaking *as* an explainer, a superior issuing an order, a peer asking a favor, a person repairing trust, a critic seeking accountability, or a negotiator preserving reciprocity. It should then test whether vocabulary, evidence, and tone actually support that role. citeturn22view7turn23view2

**Common pitfalls.** The reviewer should not confuse self-description with credible ethos, nor assume that every self-protective move is manipulative. It should also notice misalignments: a message cannot easily present itself as collaborative while repeatedly activating adversarial frames or status displays. citeturn35search5turn23view1turn23view2

**Minimal prioritized reading.** Read first: Cicero, *De Oratore*; a strong piece on Ciceronian decorum; Goffman’s *The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life* and “On Face-Work”; Lakoff on framing and value language. citeturn22view0turn27search15turn22view7turn35search5turn23view2

### Contradiction and tension detection

**Core idea.** In rhetorical review, contradiction rarely means strict logical inconsistency alone. More often it means **misfit**: between declared goal and performed act, claim and support, frame and audience, ethos and wording, or emotional intention and actual emotional cost. Cicero’s decorum and the practical orientation of rhetoric make this a central evaluative question. citeturn27search15turn26view0

**Key supporting theories or thinkers.** Toulmin makes argumentative tension inspectable by mapping claim, grounds, warrant, qualifier, backing, and rebuttal. Grice helps identify mismatch between what is said and what is implied. Orwell adds a style-thought warning: slack language encourages slack reasoning, and the process is reversible. Dewey adds the discipline of examining grounds and consequences rather than merely asserting. citeturn30view0turn28search2turn22view8turn33view2

**Essential concepts.**  
A **claim** is the main assertion; **grounds** are supporting facts; a **warrant** links grounds to claim; **backing** supports the warrant; **qualifiers** and **rebuttals** manage scope and counter-cases. A rhetorical tension exists when these levels pull against each other or when implied force undermines the ostensible purpose. citeturn30view0turn30view1turn28search0

**Conceptual role in the skill.** The reviewer should look for patterns such as: “I want feedback” that is actually a disguised demand, “I’m being objective” paired with loaded framing, or “I care about clarity” combined with euphemism, vagueness, and uninspected assumptions. It should distinguish **logical contradiction**, **pragmatic contradiction**, and **relational contradiction**. citeturn24view4turn22view8turn30view0

**Common pitfalls.** Not every variation is a contradiction. Toulmin is helpful precisely because he shows arguments are partly **field-dependent**; what counts as a warrant varies by context. Similarly, indirectness may be politeness or face-management rather than bad faith. The skill should therefore flag tensions as hypotheses with evidence, not as moral verdicts. citeturn30view1turn24view4turn35search5

**Minimal prioritized reading.** Read first: Cicero, *De Oratore*; Toulmin, *The Uses of Argument*; the SEP entry on implicature; Orwell’s “Politics and the English Language”; Dewey’s *How We Think*. citeturn22view0turn30view1turn28search0turn22view8turn33view2

### Effectiveness scoring

**Core idea.** A Ciceronian reviewer should score messages by **fitness to purpose**, not by elegance alone. The basic classical question is whether the discourse instructs, delights as needed, and moves the relevant audience in the intended direction. citeturn27search0

**Key supporting theories or thinkers.** Bitzer’s rhetoric is explicitly pragmatic and tied to effect in a rhetorical situation. Dewey’s reflective inquiry insists on grounds and consequences. Williams makes reader uptake central. Cialdini can help estimate likely compliance or responsiveness, but his principles must remain secondary and ethically bounded, because influence shortcuts are not the same as truth, fairness, or rhetorical fitness. citeturn26view0turn33view2turn22view10turn22view11

**Essential concepts.**  
In this guide, **rhetorical fitness** means the degree to which a message’s goal, act, audience, emotional cost, frame, evidence, and style fit one another and the surrounding constraints. This should be decomposed into dimensions like purpose clarity, act fit, audience fit, evidential adequacy, emotional safety, stylistic clarity, and ethical acceptability. That decomposition is a design inference supported by the traditions above, not a single inherited doctrine. citeturn26view0turn33view2turn22view10turn22view11

**Conceptual role in the skill.** The AI should produce a multi-part score or structured rating, not a monolithic number. It should separate at least three things: **persuasive potency**, **epistemic adequacy**, and **ethical fitness**. A manipulative message may score high on potency and low on ethics; a thoughtful memo may score high on adequacy and low on urgency. citeturn22view11turn33view2turn22view10

**Common pitfalls.** False precision is the main danger. Another is rewarding emotional pressure or prestige language as if these were evidence. A third is allowing “social proof” or “authority” to inflate a score that should really depend on fit, clarity, and honest support. citeturn22view11turn22view10turn22view8

**Minimal prioritized reading.** Read first: Cicero on instructing, delighting, and moving; Bitzer on rhetorical effect; Dewey on reflective inquiry; Williams on reader-centered prose; Cialdini’s official summary only as a bounded supplement on influence heuristics. citeturn27search0turn26view0turn33view2turn22view10turn22view11

### Reformulation strategy

**Core idea.** The classical anchor for revision is *De Inventione*: first find the matter, then order it, then express it fittingly. Reformulation should therefore not begin with cosmetic rewriting; it should begin with whether the message has the right goal, act, order, and style for the case. citeturn12search12

**Key supporting theories or thinkers.** Lakoff helps choose or replace frames. NVC helps turn blame into observations, judgments into feelings or needs where appropriate, and vague wishes into actionable requests. Orwell, Zinsser, and Williams add a modern style discipline: eliminate clutter, restore precision, and write in service of the reader and the situation. citeturn23view1turn23view2turn37view0turn37view1turn22view8turn22view9turn22view10

**Essential concepts.**  
A good reformulation may change **frame**, **order**, **speech-act explicitness**, **evidential support**, **face cost**, or **reader burden**. It may preserve the same goal while changing the route, or preserve the same content while changing the act being performed. The aim is not “nicer language” by default, but better rhetorical fitness. citeturn12search12turn23view1turn22view10

**Conceptual role in the skill.** The AI should propose the **smallest effective revision** first. Typical moves are: make the goal explicit, turn a disguised demand into a clear request, add grounds to a claim, remove evaluative pseudo-observations, replace opponent-framing language, reduce clutter, reorder paragraphs, or preserve necessary firmness while reducing gratuitous face threat. citeturn37view1turn23view1turn22view8turn22view10

**Common pitfalls.** Reformulation fails when it merely prettifies the surface, over-softens a legitimate boundary, erases necessary accountability, or imports a new frame that manipulates rather than clarifies. Lakoff’s distinction between honest framing and propaganda is especially important here. citeturn23view2turn22view11

**Minimal prioritized reading.** Read first: Cicero, *De Inventione* and *De Oratore*; Lakoff on frames and reframing; official NVC materials; Orwell; Zinsser’s *On Writing Well*; Williams’s *Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace*. citeturn12search12turn22view0turn23view1turn23view2turn37view0turn22view8turn22view9turn22view10

## Scaffolding instructions

Use this document as a **concept map** rather than as a fixed script. The build should preserve the workflow order—goal, act, audience, emotion, position, tension, score, reformulation—but allow later steps to revise earlier inferences when better evidence appears. That recursive structure reflects Peircean and Deweyan inquiry more accurately than a one-pass pipeline. citeturn32view0turn33view2

Keep three layers separate in the skill’s reasoning: **observed text**, **inferred intention**, and **recommended revision**. Observed text should stay close to the wording; intention should be probabilistic and evidence-linked; revision should be justified in terms of rhetorical fitness, not abstract taste. This separation is the best guard against mind-reading, overconfidence, and pseudo-psychology. citeturn37view1turn24view4turn30view0

Prefer the **least invasive successful reformulation**. If a message only needs clearer grounds, do not change its frame. If it only needs a clearer request, do not rewrite its entire tone. If a boundary must remain firm, do not force false softness. Cicero’s canons, Lakoff’s framing caution, NVC’s distinctions, and modern style teaching all point toward revisions that are precise, situational, and reader-aware rather than ornamental or doctrinaire. citeturn12search12turn23view1turn37view0turn22view8turn22view10

Finally, keep the skill’s explanations in **plain analytical English**. Use philosophical vocabulary only when it improves discrimination. The skill should sound like a careful reviewer of communicative action, not like a lecturer on rhetoric. Orwell’s warning about bad language and Williams’s reader-centered rule are good final constraints: the reviewer should make thought clearer, not more ceremonious. citeturn22view8turn22view10
