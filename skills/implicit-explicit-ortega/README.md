# Implicit Explicit Ortega

Philosopher idea: Ortega y Gasset's distinction between ideas, which we have and state, and beliefs (*creencias*), which we stand on without noticing, becomes a reading habit: separate what a piece of work says from what it stands on without saying and what it conveys without meaning to.

Practical use: use it when a request, plan, prompt, handoff, document, or skill will be read by someone without your context, when a misunderstanding came from something nobody said, or when you want the implicit made explicit. It reads the material for what it says, what it relies on without saying (deficiency), and what it implies beyond its intent (exuberance), finds unstated beliefs by their traces, and decides for each one whether to state it, cut it, leave it, or ask. It produces the explicit and implicit lists, with the exact sentences to add or cut.

## How it differs from Descartes and Aquinas

- **Audit Plan Descartes** sorts claims that are already on the table into facts and assumptions by their evidence. Ortega finds the beliefs that never became claims, so they can be stated, and then audited.
- **Grill Me Aquinas** reads the user's request for what they said, what they meant, and what happened. Ortega reads any material, including your own plan and text written for others, and also catches what a text conveys beyond what its writer meant.

## Sources

- José Ortega y Gasset, *Ideas y creencias* (1940), opening of "Creer y pensar": "Las ideas se tienen; en las creencias se está."
- That every saying is both deficient and exuberant is Ortega's idea from his later writing on language, the "nueva filología"; see Esmeralda Balaguer García, *Los límites del decir. Razón histórica y lenguaje en el último Ortega* (Tecnos, 2023), p. 46.
